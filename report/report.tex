\section{Executive Summary}
\label{sec:exec}

We apply Reinforcement Learning (RL) to ICU sepsis treatment using the
\textit{ICU-Sepsis-v2} benchmark \cite{choudhary2024}, derived from MIMIC-III
through the AI~Clinician pipeline \cite{komorowski2018}. Each episode represents
one patient trajectory; the agent selects a combined vasopressor and
intravenous-fluid dose at every step, the episode terminates in survival ($+1$) or
death ($0$), and a per-step treatment-intensity penalty ($\lambda=0.02$) discourages
unnecessary dosing. We study two configurations: a known discrete MDP
(Configuration~A, $716$ states, $25$ actions) and a continuous $47$-dimensional
clinical environment with three deployment-style failure modes (Configuration~B).

In Configuration~A, \textbf{Policy Iteration} solves the Bellman optimality
equations exactly and reaches $\mathbf{78.8\pm2.5\%}$ survival at the lowest
treatment intensity ($0.163$), confirming it as the model-based ceiling.
\textbf{Q-Learning} ($71.3\pm2.8\%$) and \textbf{SARSA} ($72.6\pm2.9\%$) beat the
random baseline ($68.7\pm2.8\%$) via temporal-difference control but trail the
exact optimum, exactly as theory predicts when sparse terminal rewards must be
back-propagated through sampled transitions. In Configuration~B, \textbf{DQN},
\textbf{PPO} and \textbf{A2C} --- trained with observation normalisation,
SOFA potential-based reward shaping and tuned hyperparameters --- match the
AI~Clinician expert policy on overall survival (DQN $67.7\pm2.9\%$, expert
$67.5\pm2.9\%$, random $64.2\pm2.9\%$) and exceed it on unperturbed episodes
($\approx74\%$ vs $71\%$). Across three training seeds the agents are stable
(DQN $65.8\pm1.0\%$, PPO $66.1\pm0.4\%$, inter-seed deviation). The DQN also
exhibits a measurable positive value-overestimation bias ($+0.194$), an empirical
demonstration of the max-operator pathology motivating Double DQN.

A central finding --- supported by an intensity--survival Pareto analysis --- is
that the sparse reward drives \emph{parsimony} rather than survival in the
unshaped setting; reward shaping resolves this by providing a dense SOFA-reduction
signal without altering the optimal policy \cite{ng1999}. All results use fixed
evaluation seeds, deterministic policies, bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals, and
Monte-Carlo simulation to avoid off-policy-evaluation biases \cite{gottesman2019}.

\section{Introduction and Problem Formalisation}
\label{sec:intro}

Sepsis is a life-threatening systemic response to infection, responsible for over
$11\%$ of global hospital deaths and a leading cause of ICU mortality
\cite{komorowski2018}. Treatment is a high-stakes sequential decision problem with
\emph{delayed consequences}: a vasopressor dose administered now may only reveal its
benefit or harm several steps later, when the patient either stabilises or
deteriorates. This structure makes sepsis management a natural application for RL.

\subsection{Markov Decision Process formulation}
\label{sec:mdp}

The problem is modelled as a discounted Markov Decision Process
$\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)$, where the state space
$\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel
$P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})$, reward function
$R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}$, and discount factor
$\gamma$ are as defined in Sutton \& Barto \cite{sutton2018}. The discounted return
from time $t$ is $G_t = \sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^k R_{t+k+1}$. The state-value and
action-value functions of a policy $\pi$ satisfy the \emph{Bellman expectation
equations}:
\begin{align}
V_\pi(s) &= \sum_a \pi(a|s)\sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\bigl[R(s,a,s')+\gamma V_\pi(s')\bigr], \\
Q_\pi(s,a) &= \sum_{s'}P(s'|s,a)\bigl[R(s,a,s')+\gamma\sum_{a'}\pi(a'|s')Q_\pi(s',a')\bigr].
\end{align}
The \emph{Bellman optimality equations} give the unique fixed point
$V^*(s)=\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s')+\gamma V^*(s')]$, from which the
optimal policy is extracted greedily.

Following the ICU-Sepsis convention we set $\gamma=1.0$: episodes are short
(approximately ten steps) and finite, so undiscounted survival probability is the
natural objective. All three configurations share this choice, making results
directly comparable.

\textbf{Actions.} The action space is $|\mathcal{A}|=25$, encoding five vasopressor
levels $v\in\{0,\ldots,4\}$ times five IV-fluid levels $f\in\{0,\ldots,4\}$:
$a=5v+f$. The normalised treatment intensity $\mathrm{INT}(a)=(v+f)/8\in[0,1]$
enters the reward as a parsimony penalty.

\textbf{Reward.} The native reward is sparse: $+1$ on survival, $0$ on death, $0$
at every intermediate step. The benchmark subtracts $\lambda\cdot\mathrm{INT}(a)$
per step ($\lambda=0.02$), encoding a preference for parsimonious care. The
initial-state distribution is biased toward sicker patients via a SOFA exponential
weight (mean start SOFA $\approx9.0$), which reduces the effective signal available
to model-free learners.

\subsection{The two configurations}
\label{sec:configs}

\textbf{Configuration~A (discrete, known MDP).} The observation is an integer
$s\in\{0,\ldots,715\}$ ($714$ clinical states $+$ two absorbing terminal states).
The full transition tensor $P\in[0,1]^{716\times25\times716}$ and reward matrix $R$
are available, so model-based dynamic programming is not only possible but optimal.
The $Q$-table has $716\times25=17{,}900$ entries, making exact tabular methods
feasible.

\textbf{Configuration~B (continuous, clinical).} The observation is the
$47$-dimensional vector of normalised physiological measurements from the
AI~Clinician dataset (SOFA, lactate, mean arterial pressure, creatinine, heart rate,
FiO$_2$, etc.). The underlying MDP dynamics are identical to Configuration~A, but
the tabular state is replaced by continuous cluster-centre features, making tabular
methods structurally infeasible: the $Q$-table would require one entry per unique
floating-point vector, i.e.\ it would be infinite. Function approximation is
therefore not a modelling choice but a \emph{necessity}. Three orthogonal failure
modes are injected to simulate deployment conditions \cite{gottesman2019}: episodic
observation noise ($p_{\text{noise}}=0.15$, Gaussian $\sigma=0.10$), episodic
missing laboratory values ($p_{\text{miss}}=0.15$, four features zeroed for the
whole episode), and rare acute events ($p_{\text{acute}}=0.01$ per step,
irreversible death independent of the treatment).

A key methodological commitment, stated once here and applied throughout, is to
report \emph{survival}, \emph{return} and \emph{treatment intensity} separately.
The summed return conflates the delayed survival signal with the accumulated
penalty; a policy can achieve a higher return than random by treating less,
without saving more patients. This separation is the only way to distinguish a
medically useful policy from a spuriously frugal one.

\section{Methodology and Algorithm Justification}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Guiding principles}

Three principles govern our choices and remain constant across both configurations.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Use the strongest method justified by available information.} Where
the model is known (Configuration~A), plan exactly with it. Where the model is
hidden (Configuration~B), use an appropriate sample-based method.
\item \textbf{Justify every design choice in terms of course theory.} Each algorithm
is selected for reasons traceable to the Bellman equations, the TD error, the
exploration-exploitation trade-off, or the function-approximation requirements.
\item \textbf{Always evaluate on the original environment.} Training interventions
(shaping, normalisation) are never allowed to contaminate evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Configuration~A: tabular algorithms and their justification}
\label{sec:methA}

Table~\ref{tab:algojust_a} summarises the algorithm selection rationale.

SARSA & Model-free on-policy TD &
  Same update structure as Q-Learning but bootstraps through the \emph{actual}

  target policy, which is clinically relevant when exploratory actions are costly. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Algorithm selection and justification for Configuration~A.}
\label{tab:algojust_a}
\end{table}

\textbf{Policy Iteration.} The algorithm alternates between policy evaluation
\begin{equation}
  V_{\pi_k}(s) \leftarrow
  \sum_{s'} P\!\left(s' \mid s, \pi_k(s)\right)
  \bigl[R(s,\pi_k(s),s') + \gamma\,V_{\pi_k}(s')\bigr]
  \quad\forall s \in \mathcal{S}
  \label{eq:pe}
\end{equation}
(iterated until $\|V_{\text{new}}-V_{\text{old}}\|_\infty<\theta=10^{-9}$) and
greedy policy improvement
$\pi_{k+1}(s)=\arg\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'|s,a)[R+\gamma V_{\pi_k}(s')]$.
By the policy improvement theorem, $V_{\pi_{k+1}}\ge V_{\pi_k}$ pointwise at every
iteration; convergence to $V^*$ is therefore guaranteed in a finite number of
steps \cite{sutton2018}. The tight tolerance ensures that any gap to model-free
methods reflects the sample-based learning challenge and not numerical looseness.

\textbf{Q-Learning} \cite{watkins1992}: the off-policy TD control update
\begin{equation}
  Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\bigl[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\bigr]
  \label{eq:ql}
\end{equation}
converges to $Q^*$ under GLIE exploration and a decreasing step-size schedule
satisfying $\sum\alpha_t=\infty$, $\sum\alpha_t^2<\infty$ \cite{sutton2018}.
We use \emph{optimistic initialisation} ($Q_0=1.0$, the maximum achievable return),
which forces the agent to explore any state--action pair that has not yet been
sufficiently visited, providing a principled substitute for the infinite exploration
required by the theoretical conditions \cite{sutton2018,ch6}.

\textbf{SARSA} \cite{rummery1994}: the on-policy TD control update
\begin{equation}
  Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\bigl[r + \gamma\,Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\bigr],
  \quad a'\sim\pi(\cdot|s')
  \label{eq:sarsa}
\end{equation}
converges under the same GLIE conditions to the value of the $\varepsilon$-greedy
policy, not to $Q^*$. Its value is clinical interpretability: because the target
accounts for the actual (exploratory) action taken next, SARSA assigns lower value
to actions that expose the patient to uncertain or costly exploratory moves. We
anneal $\varepsilon$ from $1.0$ to $0.01$ over $35{,}000$ of $50{,}000$ episodes
($\alpha=0.1$), satisfying the GLIE conditions asymptotically.

\subsection{Configuration~B: deep reinforcement learning and justification}
\label{sec:methB}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.16\textwidth}
                             >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.20\textwidth}X}
\toprule
\tH{Algorithm} & \tH{Family} & \tH{Justification} \\
\gmid
DQN \cite{mnih2015} & Value-based off-policy &
  Deep extension of Q-Learning: a neural network $Q_\theta(s,a)$ approximates
  $Q^*$ for the $47$-dimensional continuous state. Experience replay breaks
  temporal correlations; a target network stabilises the bootstrap target, which
  together address two of the three components of the \emph{deadly triad}
  (bootstrapping, off-policy, FA). The remaining risk is function approximation
  and the overestimation bias inherited from the max operator in eq.~\ref{eq:ql}. \\
PPO \cite{schulman2017} & On-policy actor-critic &
  Directly optimises $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_\pi[\sum_t\gamma^t r_t]$ via the
  clipped surrogate objective $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}=
  \mathbb{E}[\min(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\,
  \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),1{-}\epsilon,1{+}\epsilon)\hat{A}_t)]$, preventing
  destructive parameter updates. The advantage $\hat{A}_t$ is estimated via
  GAE$(\lambda)$. On-policy, so it avoids off-policy correction and the
  deadly-triad instability. \\
A2C \cite{mnih2016} & On-policy actor-critic &
  Synchronous actor-critic without PPO's clipping: the same architecture as PPO
  minus the trust-region constraint. Included as an ablation to isolate the
  contribution of the clipped objective: if PPO only improves because of clipping,
  A2C should be detectably worse; if A2C matches PPO, the bottleneck is
  reward/representation rather than the update stabiliser. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Algorithm selection and justification for Configuration~B.}
\label{tab:algojust_b}
\end{table}

All three agents use \texttt{VecNormalize} observation standardisation. The 47
clinical variables span very different scales (e.g.\ SOFA $\in[0,24]$ vs
normalised troponin), so without standardisation the early gradient steps are
dominated by scale rather than clinical relevance. We treat normalisation as
a standard preprocessing step analogous to feature scaling in supervised learning,
not as a hyperparameter.

The final report run uses $1\,000{,}000$ environment steps, Optuna-tuned
hyperparameters, and SOFA potential-based training shaping. Evaluation is always
on the unshaped default environment.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:eval}

All reported results share a single fixed protocol: $1{,}000$ evaluation episodes,
seeds drawn from a held-out list (offset 20\,000 from training seeds), deterministic
action selection (\texttt{predict(obs, deterministic=True)}), and survival read from
the terminal reward (robust to the accumulated intensity penalty). A bootstrap
$95\%$ confidence interval is computed from $2{,}000$ resamples of the per-episode
survival indicator for every reported percentage. For Configuration~B, each episode
is bucketed by failure mode using the wrappers' \texttt{info} flags, so every policy
is scored on the \emph{same set of patients}.

Using Monte-Carlo rollout in the simulator, rather than importance-weighted
off-policy estimation, sidesteps the log-data evaluation pitfalls identified by
Gottesman et al.\ \cite{gottesman2019} as the primary weakness of clinical RL.

\section{Results: Configuration~A}
\label{sec:resA}

\subsection{Main comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:configA} reports the three tabular agents on $1{,}000$ fixed-seed
evaluation episodes. Policy Iteration achieves the highest survival \emph{and} the
lowest treatment intensity, a combination that is impossible for the random policy:
random actions treat aggressively on average (intensity $0.494$) but survive less
than the learned policies.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The model-based method dominates by a wide margin ($+10.1$ pp over random, $+6.2$
pp over SARSA) and simultaneously learns a much more parsimonious policy (intensity
$0.163$ versus $0.494$ for random). The reason is structural, not algorithmic: PI
computes the full Bellman backup over the known transition matrix at every
iteration, assigning credit to early treatment decisions without sampling noise.
The model-free agents must estimate the same backed-up values from sampled
trajectories in which the survival signal appears only at the final step, creating
a severe \emph{credit-assignment} bottleneck. With only $\sim17{,}900$ state--action
pairs, $50{,}000$ episodes are enough to explore most of them, but the sparse
terminal signal still limits how precisely Q-values can be estimated.

The Q-Learning/SARSA gap is also theoretically consistent. Q-Learning evaluates the
greedy policy (the target policy) even while the behaviour policy is still
exploratory; SARSA evaluates the behaviour policy itself. Under $\varepsilon$-greedy
exploration with a treatment penalty, SARSA assigns lower value to actions that would
be taken rarely under the greedy policy but frequently during exploration, resulting
in a slightly more conservative final policy. Both effects appear in the numbers:
SARSA has higher survival than Q-Learning ($72.6\%$ vs $71.3\%$) and lower
intensity ($0.462$ vs $0.486$), without being meaningfully different in return.

The convergence curves (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:a_learning}) show that Q-Learning
and SARSA approach their respective plateaus within the $50{,}000$-episode budget,
confirming that the $\sim6$--$7$ pp gap to Policy Iteration is not a training-length
artefact but a consequence of sparse-reward credit assignment. The action
distributions (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:a_actions}) show Policy Iteration
concentrating on low-intensity action combinations, while the model-free agents
retain a more dispersed distribution that reflects residual uncertainty in the
Q-values.

additional evidence: at $300$k steps the inter-seed deviations are $\pm1.0\%$
(DQN) and $\pm0.4\%$ (PPO), fully within the intra-evaluation CI. The correct
reading is that both algorithms achieve expert-level survival, with the balance of
advantage depending on the evaluation condition.

\section{Creative Extensions}
\label{sec:ext}

The extensions form a coherent \emph{reliability and interpretability audit} of the
learned policies, structured around the three questions a clinical decision-maker
would need answered before trusting a policy: (i) is the agent optimising the right
thing, (ii) can we understand what it is doing, and (iii) does it remain credible
when the data are imperfect?

\subsection{Reward design: SOFA potential-based shaping}
\label{sec:shaping}

The unshaped reward is dominated by the treatment-intensity penalty at training
time: the sparse terminal signal appears only at episode termination, whereas the
$\lambda\cdot\mathrm{INT}(a)$ penalty arrives at every step. Gradient descent
therefore finds it easier to lower intensity than to increase survival --- both
actions raise the return under the joint reward. Empirically, without shaping, all
three deep agents hover at or below the random survival rate, learning mainly to
treat less.

We add \emph{potential-based reward shaping} \cite{ng1999} with potential
$\Phi(s)=-\beta\,\mathrm{SOFA}(s)$ and $\beta=0.05$:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{shaped}}(s,a,s') =
  R(s,a,s') + \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)
  = R(s,a,s') + \beta\bigl(\mathrm{SOFA}(s) - \gamma\,\mathrm{SOFA}(s')\bigr).
  \label{eq:shaping}
\end{equation}
By the potential-based shaping theorem \cite{ng1999}, the optimal policy is
unchanged: the shaped and original MDPs share the same $Q^*$ up to an additive
constant, so the policy extracted from the shaped agent is exactly optimal on the
original reward. Applied only at \emph{training} time, this provides a dense
organ-failure-improvement signal without altering the evaluation target. Shaping is
what lifts the agents from near-random survival to the expert-matching numbers in
Table~\ref{tab:configB}.

SOFA is the natural potential for this task: it is the clinical severity score that
defines the ICU-Sepsis benchmark, it is available in the environment's
\texttt{info} dictionary at every step, and reducing it is a plausible intermediate
signal for improving survival probability.

\subsection{Learning from demonstrations}
\label{sec:lfd}

We implement the demonstration pre-training idea of DQfD \cite{hester2018} in two
complementary settings.

\textbf{Behavioural cloning (Configuration~B).} A small two-layer MLP ($128$--$128$
units, ReLU, cross-entropy loss) is trained to classify the expert's action from
the $47$-dimensional observation, using $36{,}599$ demonstrated steps from the
AI~Clinician policy. On clean episodes BC matches the expert ($71.7\%$ vs $71.3\%$
survival, intensity $0.28$ vs $0.31$); on the full clinical distribution it falls
$\sim2$~pp below, because the supervised policy must generalise to corrupted
observations at test time while the expert acts on the underlying true state. This
gap directly quantifies the \emph{observation-degradation cost} introduced by the
failure modes.

\textbf{Expert Q warm-start (Configuration~A).} The tabular $Q$-table is
initialised from expert demonstrations via Monte-Carlo returns before Q-Learning
begins. The cold agent ($Q_0=0$, or equivalently $Q_0=1$ without the warm-start)
stalls near the random plateau and never exceeds $70\%$ survival in $15{,}000$
episodes ($68.8\%$, intensity $0.452$). The warm-started agent escapes the plateau,
reaches $70\%$ by episode $\sim9{,}000$ and finishes at $\mathbf{71.9\%}$ with a
markedly more parsimonious policy (intensity $0.345$). The gain is purely due to the
starting point: the algorithm, the step size, the exploration schedule, and the
environment are unchanged. Prior clinical structure therefore reduces the
exploration burden imposed by sparse rewards without replacing RL.

\subsection{Interpretability: permutation importance and SHAP}
\label{sec:interp}

Two complementary attribution methods are applied to the trained DQN. Permutation
importance measures the mean absolute change in the greedy-action $Q$-value when
each of the $47$ features is individually shuffled across $500$ sampled states.
SHAP \cite{lundberg2017} uses the \texttt{GradientExplainer} on the same $Q$-network
to compute theoretically grounded feature contributions. Both methods rank
established sepsis-severity markers (lactate, respiratory rate, urine output,
creatinine) among the five most decision-relevant features, lending clinical
plausibility to the policy.

Permutation importance is reported with an explicit caveat: single-feature
permutation produces off-manifold inputs on correlated physiological variables, and
can therefore overestimate the importance of features that are correlated with
high-importance ones \cite{hooker2021}. Reporting both methods, and highlighting
that their rankings agree, provides stronger evidence than either alone.

\subsection{Q-value overestimation diagnostic}
\label{sec:overest}

Q-Learning's max operator causes a systematic positive bias in value estimates when
the $Q$-function is approximated: the learner consistently selects the action with
the largest estimated value, which tends to be the one whose error is most positive
\cite{vanhasselt2016}. Comparing the DQN's predicted greedy-action value against
the realised Monte-Carlo return over $300$ evaluation episodes:
\[
  \bar{Q}_\theta = 0.830, \quad \bar{G}_{\text{MC}} = 0.636,
  \quad \text{bias} = +0.194 \;\;(\approx 30\%).
\]
The scatter plot (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:b_overest}) shows that virtually all
points lie above the calibration diagonal, confirming the systematic upward bias
predicted by theory and motivating Double DQN as future work.

\subsection{Treatment intensity versus survival: the Pareto frontier}
\label{sec:pareto}

The title figure plots every trained policy in the (intensity, survival) plane. It
is the most compact clinical summary of the project: Policy Iteration achieves the
highest survival at the lowest intensity and is therefore Pareto-dominant; the
random policy is Pareto-dominated by every learned policy (it treats more and
survives less); within Configuration~B, A2C occupies the low-intensity corner,
DQN the high-survival corner, and the expert, PPO and BC fall between them. This
framing makes the purpose of the $\lambda$ penalty explicit: it shifts the Pareto
frontier by rewarding parsimony, and the \emph{position} of a policy on the
frontier is a more informative summary than its scalar return.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\textbf{Why the random baseline is high.}
\textit{ICU-Sepsis-v2} is designed around real MIMIC-III trajectories in which
most patients survive, so the action has a small marginal effect relative to the
patient's initial state. Policy Iteration confirms the ceiling: $78.8\%$, only
$\sim10$~pp above random. This is not a weakness of the benchmark but a clinical
reality of sepsis management. Any claim of $\approx90\%$ survival would indicate
an altered environment rather than a better agent.

\textbf{Parsimony versus survival under sparse rewards.}
The reward conflates the sparse terminal survival signal with the dense per-step
intensity penalty. Without shaping, gradient descent finds the path of least
resistance: lower intensity. The shaped agents instead receive an SOFA-reduction
signal at every step, redirecting the gradient toward the clinically meaningful
outcome. This finding is the most important practical contribution of the project.

\textbf{Theoretical consistency.}
The project instantiates the full arc of the course. Dynamic programming (Policy
Iteration, Bellman backup), on-policy and off-policy TD control (SARSA,
Q-Learning, TD error), value-function approximation and the deadly triad (DQN,
experience replay, target network), policy-gradient and actor-critic methods with
clipping (PPO, A2C, advantage estimation), the overestimation bias ($+0.194$
measured), reward design with policy invariance (potential-based shaping), and
multi-seed reliability reporting \cite{henderson2018} are all present and
explicitly linked to the corresponding results.

\textbf{Configuration~A versus Configuration~B.}
The survival drop from $78.8\%$ (PI, A) to $67.7\%$ (DQN, B) is not evidence that
deep RL is generally worse than tabular RL. It reflects three simultaneous changes:
the model is hidden, the state is replaced by a noisy high-dimensional vector, and
three failure modes inject irreducible uncertainty. Under the full clinical
environment the agents reach expert-level performance, which is the meaningful
result.

\textbf{Clinical reading.}
An RL policy for sepsis is valuable not merely because it recommends treatment, but
because it balances outcome and treatment burden under uncertainty. Policy Iteration
demonstrates that the Pareto-optimal balance exists: high survival with low
intensity. The deep agents recover a similar balance in the harder continuous
setting, with DQN emphasising survival and A2C emphasising parsimony. The on-policy
PPO occupies the middle ground, consistent with the expectation that clipped
policy-gradient updates are conservative in a noisy environment.


% ============================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}

The Optuna search is a proxy at $50$k steps per trial; the final hyperparameters
are near-optimal but not globally optimal. The multi-seed reliability study uses
$300$k steps for tractability and confirms stability but does not replace the
$1$M headline run. The simulator is derived from observational MIMIC-III data and
encodes the AI~Clinician's modelling assumptions; results are not transferable to
bedside without prospective validation. The failure-mode wrappers are simplified
stress tests: real ICU missingness is informative and workflow-correlated, not a
random subset of zeroed features.

Three concrete extensions are proposed: (i) \textbf{Double DQN} to test whether
decoupling action selection from evaluation eliminates the measured $+0.194$
overestimation bias; (ii) a \textbf{$\beta$ sensitivity sweep} of the SOFA shaping
coefficient to trace the parsimony--survival Pareto frontier under different
reward-design choices; (iii) a \textbf{full-budget CIEDE-style SOFA run} at
$1$M steps from multiple seeds to confirm that the shaping advantage is stable
rather than an artefact of a single training seed.

All reported results are on a simulated benchmark. \textbf{They are not clinical
advice and must not be used for bedside decision support.}


% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

On the discrete known-MDP setting of ICU-Sepsis, model-based Policy Iteration
(Bellman optimality, exact DP) achieves $78.8\%$ survival at the lowest treatment
intensity, setting a model-based ceiling. Tabular Q-Learning and SARSA beat the
random baseline via temporal-difference control but trail the ceiling, as
credit-assignment theory under sparse rewards predicts. In the harder
continuous-observation clinical setting, DQN, PPO and A2C --- with observation
normalisation, SOFA potential-based shaping and tuned hyperparameters --- match the
AI~Clinician expert on overall survival and exceed it on clean episodes, with
inter-seed stability confirmed over three seeds. The central finding is that
unshaped deep RL learns parsimony rather than survival; potential-based shaping
resolves this without altering the optimal policy. A clinical reliability suite ---
reward design, learning from demonstrations, SHAP interpretability, and an
overestimation diagnostic --- grounds the technical results in clinical meaning and
covers the theoretical and practical content of the reinforcement learning course.


% ============================================================
\section*{Note on statistical methodology}

All pairwise survival comparisons use overlapping bootstrap $95\%$ confidence
intervals as the primary test. The same evaluation seeds are shared across all
policies, so per-policy differences cannot be caused by patient-mix variation.
For the multi-seed study, the relevant variance is the inter-seed standard deviation
of the mean survival ($\pm1.0\%$ for DQN, $\pm0.4\%$ for PPO), which is smaller
than the intra-evaluation CI, supporting the reliability claim without a formal
hypothesis test.
